# A Short History of Jazz

Jazz emerged in New Orleans at the turn of the twentieth century, a fusion
of African rhythmic traditions, European harmonic language, blues, and
ragtime. It is often described as America's only original art form,
although that claim underplays the African roots that made it possible.

## New Orleans and Early Jazz

The first generation of jazz musicians played in New Orleans brass bands
and in the brothels and bars of the Storyville district. Buddy Bolden,
working around 1900, is often cited as the first jazz cornetist, though
no recording of him survives. Jelly Roll Morton, Louis Armstrong, and
Sidney Bechet all came out of this scene. Armstrong's 1925-1928 Hot Five
and Hot Seven recordings shifted the music from a collective polyphony to
an art centered on a virtuoso soloist improvising over chord changes.

## Swing and the Big Bands

In the 1930s jazz became popular dance music, played by big bands under
leaders like Duke Ellington, Count Basie, and Benny Goodman. Arrangements
became more important, sections of the band traded riffs, and soloists
like Coleman Hawkins and Lester Young extended the vocabulary of the
saxophone. Ellington, in particular, composed music specifically for the
individual voices of his sidemen, treating the orchestra as a palette.

## Bebop

After World War II a younger generation led by Charlie Parker and Dizzy
Gillespie developed bebop: fast tempos, complex chord substitutions, and
improvisation based on scales and upper extensions of chords rather than
just the melody. Bebop moved jazz from dance halls to listening clubs.
Thelonious Monk contributed a harmonic sensibility of deliberate
dissonance that still sounds modern.

## Cool, Hard Bop, and Modal

In the 1950s Miles Davis led a reaction against bebop's density with the
Birth of the Cool sessions. Hard bop, played by Art Blakey's Jazz
Messengers and Horace Silver, brought the gospel and blues back. In 1959
Miles Davis's Kind of Blue introduced modal jazz: improvisation on a few
scales sustained for long stretches, rather than on rapidly shifting
chords. It remains the best-selling jazz album in history.

## Free Jazz and After

Ornette Coleman's 1959 album The Shape of Jazz to Come and John Coltrane's
late work broke with fixed chord progressions entirely, opening the door
to free jazz. From the 1970s onward jazz fragmented into fusion with rock,
Latin-jazz, the acoustic revival led by Wynton Marsalis, and a
contemporary global scene that still treats improvisation as the center
of the music.
