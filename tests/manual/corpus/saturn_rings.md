# The Rings of Saturn

Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second-largest in the Solar
System. Its most striking feature is an extensive system of rings made
almost entirely of water ice with trace amounts of rocky material and dust.

## Structure

The main rings, labeled from inside out, are the D, C, B, A, F, G, and E
rings. The B ring is the brightest and most massive; the A and B rings are
separated by the Cassini Division, a 4,800 km gap that appears dark when
viewed from Earth. The F ring, discovered by Pioneer 11 in 1979, is narrow
and braided, shepherded by the small moons Prometheus and Pandora.

The rings span from about 7,000 km to 80,000 km above Saturn's equator, yet
they are remarkably thin: typically only 10 to 100 meters in vertical
extent. If the rings were scaled to the diameter of a soccer field, they
would be thinner than a sheet of paper.

## Composition

Spectroscopic measurements from the Cassini mission, which orbited Saturn
from 2004 to 2017, showed that the ring particles are 90 to 95 percent
water ice. Particle sizes range from dust grains smaller than a millimeter
to boulders several meters across. The rings are bright because water ice
reflects sunlight efficiently.

## Origin and Age

The origin of the rings is debated. One hypothesis is that they are the
debris of a moon torn apart by tidal forces after straying inside Saturn's
Roche limit. Another suggests they formed from material left over when
Saturn coalesced from the solar nebula 4.5 billion years ago. Recent
Cassini data on the mass and contamination of the rings suggest they may be
much younger than Saturn itself, perhaps only 100 to 400 million years old,
meaning dinosaurs on Earth may have predated them.

## Visibility

The rings are tilted 26.7 degrees relative to Saturn's orbit around the
Sun. As Saturn orbits the Sun every 29.5 Earth years, the rings appear to
open and close from our viewpoint. Twice per orbit they are seen edge-on
and nearly disappear, most recently in 2025. Galileo first saw them in
1610 but his telescope was too weak to resolve them as rings; he described
Saturn as having "ears." Christiaan Huygens correctly identified them as a
disk in 1655.
