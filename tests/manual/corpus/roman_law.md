# Foundations of Roman Law

Roman law is the legal system of ancient Rome, from the Twelve Tables of
451 BC through the Corpus Juris Civilis of Justinian in the sixth century
AD. It forms the historical basis of most civil law systems in continental
Europe, Latin America, and parts of Asia and Africa.

## The Twelve Tables

The Twelve Tables (Leges Duodecim Tabularum) were the first written code
of Roman law, produced by a commission of ten men, the decemviri, around
451-449 BC. They were engraved on bronze tablets and posted in the Forum
so that the plebeians could see the rules that bound them. Before the
tables, law was customary and known only to patrician priests, who could
interpret it to their own advantage.

## Ius Civile and Ius Gentium

Roman jurists distinguished several bodies of law. Ius civile was the law
applicable to Roman citizens. Ius gentium was the law of nations,
applicable to foreigners and to disputes between Romans and non-Romans; it
emerged from the need to handle the increasing commerce of an expanding
empire. Ius naturale, natural law, was a philosophical idea borrowed from
the Stoics that there exist rules inherent in nature and reason,
applicable to all creatures.

## Praetors and the Edict

The praetor was a magistrate elected annually. At the start of his term he
published an edict (edictum) stating how he would exercise his
jurisdiction. Over time the edicts became cumulative and, in effect, a
body of case law. The emperor Hadrian ordered the jurist Salvius Julianus
around 130 AD to produce a perpetual edict (edictum perpetuum) that fixed
the text and ended the praetor's legislative role.

## The Corpus Juris Civilis

The emperor Justinian commissioned a comprehensive compilation of Roman
law between 529 and 534 AD. The resulting Corpus Juris Civilis has four
parts: the Codex (imperial enactments), the Digest or Pandects (excerpts
from classical jurists), the Institutes (a textbook for students), and
the Novellae (new laws issued by Justinian after the Codex). The Digest
preserves the opinions of jurists such as Ulpian, Paulus, and Gaius that
would otherwise have been lost.

## Influence

Roman law was rediscovered in northern Italy in the eleventh century when
scholars at the University of Bologna began systematic study of the
Digest. The tradition that grew out of their glosses and commentaries,
known as the ius commune, shaped European legal thought down to the
codifications of the nineteenth century, most famously the French Code
Civil of 1804 and the German BGB of 1900.
